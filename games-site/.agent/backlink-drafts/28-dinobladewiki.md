Keyword research pushes you toward fragmentation. Five related queries look like five pages. Five pages about one system is how a wiki becomes a maze where every answer is 60% of the answer.

The unit that works is the system, not the query.

## Why fragmentation fails

Take a game mechanic with several parts: what it does, how you unlock it, how it scales, and what it interacts with. Split that across four pages and each one has to explain the other three in order to make sense — usually badly, and inconsistently, because they were written at different times.

The reader notices immediately: they read one page, click to another for the missing half, and land on a page that contradicts the first.

## The boundary test

Two questions decide whether something deserves its own page:

1. **Does it stand alone?** Can a reader who lands on it cold get a complete answer without the other pages?
2. **Would the page be worse if merged?** If merging would make a 5,000-word page with three unrelated questions, keep them apart.

If a section fails (1) and passes (2), it is a section, not a page. That is most of them.

## What to do with the queries you did not create pages for

Use them as headings. A page with an H2 phrased as the query — "how do you unlock soul arts" — answers the same search intent while keeping the explanation in one place, and the snippet can still be drawn from that section.

This is why headings matter more than most people treat them: on a well-structured page, the section *is* the targeted page, without the split.

## What actually deserves its own page

- A different system, with its own mechanics and its own vocabulary.
- A reference table that people return to repeatedly.
- A question that is genuinely independent — a specific puzzle, a specific boss.

Everything else is a section.

The [how soul arts work in Dinoblade](https://dinobladewiki.vercel.app/guides/soul-arts-guide) page on [Dinoblade Wiki](https://dinobladewiki.vercel.app) is one page for one system for exactly this reason: unlocking, scaling, and interactions are three questions with one answer, and splitting them would have produced three pages that each needed the other two to be useful.
