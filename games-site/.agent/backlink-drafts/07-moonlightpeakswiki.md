I write guides for small games. The pages I enjoy writing are the ones about systems — how the crafting loop works, why the combat feels the way it does. Those pages get almost no traffic.

The pages that get traffic are the ones answering "how do I get more money, right now, in the first two hours". Which is not an interesting question. It is just the question most players type.

## What the data actually shows

Across the sites I run, the shape repeats:

- **One or two pages carry most of the organic traffic.** Usually a "how to make money / how to get stronger early" page or a tier list.
- **The rest of the site exists to make those pages credible and to catch long-tail queries.** They are not wasted, but they are not the engine either.
- **The median page gets very little.** Which is fine — that is what a distribution looks like — but it means "average traffic per page" is a useless planning metric.

The practical consequence: if you are deciding where to spend the next hour, the answer is usually a better version of the page that already gets traffic, not a new page.

## Why "make money" pages win

Three reasons, none of them about writing quality:

1. **The intent is universal.** Every player needs resources. Not every player cares about your analysis of the crafting system.
2. **The query is unbranded.** Someone searches the mechanic, not the game name — which means the page is not competing only against the game's own wiki.
3. **The answer is verifiable.** A page that says "here are three routes, here is what each one costs you in time" can be judged correct or wrong. Verifiable pages get shared and linked.

That third one is the only part under your control, and it is where most money guides fail: they list methods without saying what each one costs you. A list of ten ways to earn is less useful than three, each with a time estimate.

## The structure I use

- **Answer first.** Forty to sixty words at the top with the single best route, before any context.
- **Then the alternatives, each with a cost.** Time, setup, risk of patch changes.
- **Then the edge cases.** What stops working at a certain point in the game, what needs a specific item.
- **Then the honest caveat.** If a route is only good early, say so. Pages that pretend every method stays viable lose trust fast.

The [page on how to make money early in Moonlight Peaks](https://moonlightpeakswiki.vercel.app/guides/money-guide) on [Moonlight Peaks Wiki](https://moonlightpeakswiki.vercel.app) follows that shape, and it is the site's most-read page by a wide margin — not because it is the best writing on the site, but because it answers the question people actually ask, first.

## The uncomfortable planning implication

If one page type pays for the rest, then a content plan built on "20 pages per site, evenly" is misallocated. A better plan looks like:

- 1–2 pages of the money/tier-list type, written properly, kept current;
- 5–8 pages that support them with internal links and real answers;
- a long tail of specific questions, added when search data says someone is asking.

Which is roughly what my sites converge on over time — usually after I have spent a month on the pages I enjoy instead. The tooling and the templates are easy to get right. The hard part is admitting which page pays for the rest, and then treating it like the most important page on the site instead of the least interesting one.
