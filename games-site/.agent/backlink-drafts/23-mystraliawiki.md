Three strings do three jobs on every page:

- the **slug**, which is a URL — short, stable, and expensive to change;
- the **title tag**, which appears in a search result — 40–60 characters, persuasive;
- the **H1**, which is the first thing a reader sees — can be a sentence.

Most sites derive all three from one value, and then wonder why the page underperforms. Each job has different constraints, and a string that satisfies one usually fails the others.

## The slug is permanent

`/guides/builds` and `/guides/best-spell-builds` rank for different things, and changing a slug breaks every inbound link, every bookmark, and every internal reference. So the slug is chosen once and then left alone.

The rule I use: short, lowercase, keyword-bearing, and rare enough not to collide with a future page. Not the full title — a URL is a location, not a headline.

## The title is a competitive claim

It has to work in a list of ten results, next to competitors, with the tail possibly truncated. That means:

- the identifying words early, not the brand;
- a reason to click, not just a topic;
- honesty about scope — if the page covers early game only, say so, because the wrong click bounces.

## The H1 can be the answer

The H1 is the only one of the three a reader reads *inside* the page, and it is the best place to restate the question they arrived with. It can be longer than the title, and it should not be a repeat of the brand plus the topic.

## Where the three usually fail together

The common failure is an undifferentiated slug like `/guides/guide-3`, a title like "Spell Builds | Site Name", and an H1 that repeats the title. Three chances to communicate, one message, none of them doing the job.

The second common failure is the opposite: a clickbait title with an H1 that does not match it. That gets the click and loses the read, which on a small site is worse than not getting the click.

## A cheap habit

Before publishing, read the three strings in sequence:

1. A URL someone could type or share.
2. A search result that stands out without lying.
3. A heading that answers the reader's question.

If any of the three is a duplicate of another, it is not doing its job.
