# How to Use the Silver Pines Camera — Photo Map Notes and the 100-Photo Limit

Silver Pines gives you a camera early and then barely explains it. That is a mistake waiting to happen, because the camera is not a cosmetic photo mode. It is the game's **in-world note-taking system**: a way to record a locked door, a keypad, a poster or a puzzle clue so that your map remembers it for you.

The system became more generous in **Hotfix 2** on **October 16, 2026**, which raised the maximum photo storage from **30 to 100** [Official]. That change makes the camera practical for a full playthrough rather than a novelty you use for the first few hours and then abandon.

This guide covers where the camera comes from, how photos become map notes, what is worth photographing, and the photo-management habits that keep the limit from becoming a nuisance.

## Where to Get the Camera

The camera is in the **trunk of Red Walker's car** at the start of the game. After you wake up, take the car keys from the table, use the payphone outside to save, and open the trunk. Inside you collect **your case files, the camera and your notes** [Community, Games.gg].

That placement is easy to miss if you treat the trunk as a story prop. The camera is part of the opening kit, not a later unlock. Pick it up before you leave the area, because the first locked doors and clue-bearing rooms are exactly what the camera is for.

The opening also gives you a free coin from the payphone's coin return slot. Use it to save before you start exploring; Silver Pines has no autosave, and the [save system guide](/guides/silver-pines-save-system-phone-booths) explains why that first call matters.

## How a Photo Becomes a Map Note

The camera's workflow is simple, but the important part is what happens after the shutter:

1. **Frame the thing you cannot solve yet.** A locked door, a keypad, a plaque, a poster, a wall symbol, a radio dial.
2. **Take the photo.** The camera prints the shot after capture [Community, NPC Tips].
3. **Add the photo to your map.** The photo can be attached to the map at the location where it was taken, so the map carries the visual reminder [Community, NPC Tips].

That last step is the whole point. A screenshot on your desktop is outside the game. A photo on the map is a note that appears exactly where you will need it when you come back with the right tool.

One caution: the map in Silver Pines **runs in real time** and does not pause the game [Community-reported]. The same is true of the inventory and other menus. Adding a photo is therefore not a safe action in the middle of a fight. Do it in a cleared room, behind a closed door, or near a save.

## What Is Worth Photographing

The camera is most useful for information you cannot carry in your head across a long metroidvania session. Prioritize:

- **Locked doors and blocked paths** — the classic "come back with a tool" marker.
- **Keypads and code panels** — photograph the panel, then any clue that might explain it.
- **Paintings, plaques and posters with numbers or symbols** — these are frequently part of a puzzle chain.
- **Radio settings and dial layouts** — one late-game puzzle asks you to match a truck radio to a police radio; photographing the original settings saves a return trip.
- **Wall symbols near phone booths** — the finale uses photographed symbols to solve a telephone puzzle.
- **Anything that looks like a diagram** — a map fragment, a chart, a sequence of icons.

Do not photograph every room. The limit is now 100, which is generous, but a camera roll full of duplicate corridors makes the important notes harder to find. Photograph **the unsolved thing and its clue**, not the scenery.

## Worked Example: The Sheriff's Office Safe

The Sheriff's Office is the clearest demonstration of how the camera is meant to be used. The safe is not opened by a single code you find on a note. It is opened by **collecting four photographs and comparing them with a reference book** [Community, Showgamer].

The four photos are:

1. The **reception/lobby wall** image.
2. The **basement archive** image.
3. The **wall outside the morgue's refrigerated room**.
4. The **stone plaque in the garden**, south of the dog cages.

The camera records the images. The sheriff's book supplies the reference. The manual page supplies the dial directions. The resulting safe code is **15 → 7 → 3 → 10**, turned **clockwise to 15, counterclockwise to 7, clockwise to 3, counterclockwise to 10** [Community, Showgamer].

The camera does not unlock the safe by itself. That is the design: it gives you a portable, map-linked copy of the clue so you can do the comparison without running back and forth. If you photograph the four images and then wonder why the safe will not open, the missing step is the book and the dial sequence, not the photos.

The same pattern repeats elsewhere. A police cruiser's radio layout can be photographed and later matched on Highway 13 to receive the Sheriff's Office entry code **1715**. A poster of Father Jacob Foster is photographed on Church Hill and used as the necklace-symbol reference for the Chapel Tower cube puzzle. Near the finale, the strange symbols on the wall are photographed to solve the telephone puzzle [Community, Showgamer].

## The 100-Photo Limit and How to Manage It

Before Hotfix 2, the camera held **30 photos**. That is enough for the opening area and not much else, especially if you photograph every locked door you pass. Hotfix 2 raised the cap to **100** [Official, Noisy Pixel].

The camera itself has **unlimited uses**; the cap is on stored photos [Community-reported]. You never waste film — only storage.

A practical deletion policy:

- **Delete solved clues.** Once a safe is open, the four photos that solved it are dead weight unless you want them for a guide.
- **Keep unsolved doors and codes.** Those are the notes that earn their space.
- **Keep one photo per puzzle chain, not every angle.** If you already have a clear shot of the keypad, you do not need three more.
- **Delete duplicates before deleting unique clues.** A duplicate corridor is cheaper to lose than the only photo of a symbol you have not identified.
- **Do not delete during a dangerous room.** The menu does not pause the game. Clean up at a save point.

If you are the kind of player who takes a photo of every interesting wall, 100 will still fill up. The fix is not a bigger cap; it is treating the camera as a **to-do list**, not a scrapbook.

## Camera vs External Screenshots

You can always take a screenshot with your platform's capture button. The in-game camera is better for one reason: **the note lives on the map**. It is attached to the location, so when you return to the area, the reminder is already there. An external screenshot folder cannot do that without you organizing it yourself.

The trade-off is that the camera's photos may be lower resolution or more awkward to frame than a platform screenshot, but the map link usually outweighs that.

## Common Camera Mistakes

1. **Leaving the camera in the trunk.** It is part of the opening kit. Pick it up before you explore.
2. **Taking the photo but not adding it to the map.** The photo alone is just an image. The map note is the actual tool.
3. **Photographing the door but not the clue.** A picture of a locked door tells you it is locked. A picture of the keypad and the nearby plaque tells you what to do about it.
4. **Deleting a clue before you have used it.** The limit is 100 now, but the habit still matters.
5. **Opening the camera in combat.** Menus do not pause the game. Take photos in safe rooms.
6. **Assuming the camera solves puzzles automatically.** It records information. You still have to compare, decode and act.

## What Is Still Unconfirmed

- The exact **control** for raising the camera on keyboard and controller.
- Whether photos are stored **per save file** or shared across saves.
- The camera's **resolution limit** and whether photo quality affects any puzzle.
- Whether future patches will change the **100-photo cap** again.

None of those gaps changes the core habit: photograph what you cannot solve, attach it to the map, and keep the roll clean. The camera is one of the few systems in Silver Pines that makes the game easier without spending a coin.
