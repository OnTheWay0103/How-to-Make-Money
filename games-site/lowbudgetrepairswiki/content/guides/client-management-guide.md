# Low-Budget Repairs Client Management — Handling Tenants, Requests & Complaints

The clients of Low-Budget Repairs are the neighbors and local characters of a 1990s Polish apartment block — people who want their flats fixed for as little money as possible, and who are exactly as observant as they need to be. Managing them is half the game: read their requests right, pass their inspections, survive their complaints, and they become your repeat business. Based on the July 2026 demo; launch is **August 13, 2026**.

## Reading the Request: Required vs. Optional

Every contract is a request with a hidden structure. The most valuable client-management skill is reading it:

- **Required work** — surfaces and fixes the contract names. These are inspected. Skipping one fails the job.
- **Optional work** — everything else the client might *like* done but didn't ask for. Not inspected, not paid. Never do it.
- **Wording matters.** "Refurbish the worktop" means paint and clean it — buying a new worktop is your mistake, not the client's requirement. "Replace the bathtub" is explicit and expensive — know before you bid. The [renovation jobs guide](/guides/renovation-jobs-guide) breaks down what each contract type actually demands.

When a request is ambiguous, the demo's pattern is: **the narrowest reading wins.** If the client wanted more, they would have written it down.

## The Inspection Is the Relationship

Every contract ends with the client walking the flat and judging your work with their own eyes — the full mechanics are in our [inspection guide](/guides/inspection-guide). For client management, three things matter:

1. **First impressions set the tone.** The client enters through the entrance hall and moves room by room. A clean, well-painted first room puts them in a paying mood; a messy first room makes them look for problems. Sequence your best work toward the front of the flat (see [upgrade priorities](/guides/upgrade-priorities-guide)).
2. **Clients check what they can see.** They don't move furniture, open closets, or measure angles. If a flaw is invisible from the doorway, it doesn't exist — this is the entire basis of [cost-cutting](/guides/cost-cutting-tips).
3. **Cleanliness is part of the job.** Paint drips, open cans, and debris read as unprofessional before the client even looks at the walls. Five minutes of tidying before the walkthrough is the cheapest "client service" in the game.

## What Happens When They Complain

The demo shows three outcomes, not two:

- **Full pass** — payment in full, grumbles minimal.
- **Pass with grumbling** — minor visible issues (a streak, a scuff). The client makes a face and hands over the fee anyway. This is the state to aim for: the money is the same, and you saved the polish.
- **Fail** — required work unfinished, a leak, or serious visible damage. No payment. Depending on the contract, the client may demand a redo — meaning you fix the specific problem for free, on your own materials.

The fail rules for a client manager:

- **A redo only covers what they caught.** Fix exactly that, cheaply, and the grumbling pass follows. Don't "improve" anything else — you're not getting paid for it.
- **Never argue with the tap test.** Plumbing is the one behavioral check: the client turns the tap, and if it drips, nothing else you did matters. Re-test your own plumbing before calling the client — a 20-second check saves an entire redo.
- **Don't stack shortcuts on visible work.** One cheap trick on a wall passes; five obvious mistakes on the same wall get noticed. Spread the risk: posters behind furniture, not on the show wall.

## Reputation and Repeat Clients

The demo shows the start of a reputation loop: **complete jobs successfully and more contracts appear.** What's known:

- **Failing jobs sets you back.** Botched inspections reduce future job availability — reputation, not a score, controls your job board.
- **Neighbors watch you work.** The block is a small community; clients talk. A visible disaster in one flat means fewer offers from its neighbors.
- **Repeat clients are the prize.** The same tenants need the same rooms again — and a client who paid once with grumbling will pay again, faster, on a smaller scope. Regulars are your cash flow.

That's the client-management strategy in one sentence: **pass inspections cheaply, never fail the same client twice, and let the neighborhood feed you contracts.**

## When to Decline a Client

Turning down work is a management decision, not a failure. Decline when:

1. **The client demands quality.** Some requests effectively require premium materials — if the fee can't cover them profitably, the math fails ([job profit guide](/guides/job-profit-guide)).
2. **The scope is a trap.** Big scope with a small fee, or repairs you can't cheaply fake (new floorboards, structural work). A failed 100% job pays worse than a declined one.
3. **The store can't supply it.** No bin paint, no usable tiles — a full-price job is a 30% margin job. Wait for restock.
4. **You've already failed them once.** The redo client is a debt; taking another risky job from the same tenant multiplies the damage when it fails.

## Communication Do's and Don'ts

The game has no dialogue system — "communication" is what you leave behind in the flat:

- **Do** finish visible work before the client arrives; first impressions carry the inspection.
- **Do** leave the flat tidier than you found it (in the visible areas, anyway).
- **Do** re-test everything you can trigger (taps, doors, drawers) before calling the client.
- **Don't** leave your tools and cans where the client walks.
- **Don't** paint yourself into a corner with a second visit — clients who wait get impatient, and impatience reads as suspicion.

Manage the clients right and the money follows: their complaints stay at grumble level, their fees land in your pocket, and their neighbors become your next job board.
