# Low-Budget Repairs Inspection Guide — How Clients Judge Your Work

> Based on the July 2026 demo. The inspection system may evolve in the full release (August 13, 2026) — this page will be updated with verified release-build details after launch.

## How the Inspection Works

Every contract in Low-Budget Repairs ends the same way: **the client walks the flat and looks at your work.** You're not graded by a computer and there's no "quality score" popup — the client simply moves through the rooms, eyes the surfaces, and reacts.

The demo reveals the system's most important trait: **the inspection is surface-level.** Clients judge what they can see from standing height with the lights on. They don't open cabinets, they don't move furniture, and they don't measure angles. If a problem is invisible from the doorway, for the purposes of getting paid, it doesn't exist.

That one fact drives every strategy in the game, and it's why our [cost-cutting tips guide](/guides/cost-cutting-tips) works at all.

## What Clients Check

Across the demo's contracts, the inspection covers five things:

### 1. Paint Coverage

The most-checked item. Every wall the contract requires must look painted. Unpainted walls are an automatic fail; streaky or thin walls usually pass. Clients notice: bare patches, walls of a completely different color, and paint lines that stop halfway.

The [painting guide](/guides/painting-guide) covers how little paint actually satisfies this check.

### 2. Tile Alignment and Coverage

Tile work is checked from standing height — clients notice missing tiles, unfilled gaps, and visibly crooked rows. They rarely notice minor grout inconsistencies or a slightly uneven floor, and they never measure. Our [tiling guide](/guides/tiling-guide) explains which shortcuts survive this check.

### 3. Plumbing Function

This is the only category with a *behavioral* test: the client turns the tap. A drip, a leak, or an actively spraying joint fails the job no matter how clean everything looks. Function here is binary — if water goes where it should and stops when turned off, the plumbing passes. Duct tape counts, as long as it actually holds.

### 4. Visible Damage

Holes, cracks, water stains, and broken fixtures in plain sight all catch the client's eye. The cheap answer is never repair — it's *cover*: posters over holes, paint over stains, caulk over cracks. Covered damage passes; uncovered damage fails. When covering isn't possible, the cheapest visible fix wins.

### 5. Cleanliness

Clients notice the mess you leave behind: paint drips on the floor, debris, open cans, tools lying around. A messy flat reads as unprofessional and sets the client's mood before they even look at the walls. Five minutes of tidying before the inspection is the cheapest insurance policy in the game.

## Pass / Fail Criteria

There's no official pass/fail rubric in the game, but the demo's behavior is consistent:

- **Pass:** every required surface is covered, no obviously damaged or leaky areas, flat reasonably tidy. Payment in full.
- **Partial pass:** minor visible issues (a streak here, a scuff there) usually still pay — clients grumble but hand over the fee.
- **Fail:** a required surface unfinished, a leak, or serious visible damage. No payment — and worse, see below.

The distinction between "pass with grumbling" and "fail" is why doing the *visible* work well while skipping everything else is the optimal strategy.

## What Happens When You Fail

Failing an inspection costs more than the job:

- **No payment.** The flat fee is gone. Any supplies you bought came out of your own pocket, so a failed job is a net loss, not just a missed paycheck.
- **A redo may be demanded.** The client asks you to fix the specific problem they spotted. You keep working without pay until it's right.
- **Reputation damage.** Demo players report that botched inspections reduce future job offers — the neighbors talk. Fail too often and the easy, profitable contracts stop appearing.
- **Time wasted.** Every failed inspection is an hour you could have spent on a paying job. See the [money-making guide](/guides/money-making-guide) for how compounding profits work.

## The Contract: Your Real Permit

Every job runs through two gates: the **contract** that defines what you're allowed (and paid) to do, and the **inspection** that decides whether you get paid at all. The closest thing the demo has to a permit system is the **contract itself** — a flat-fee agreement in which the client names the work, the budget, and the deadline, and you decide how to spend the money:

- **Read the contract first (Tab).** It names the required surfaces — walls, floors, fixtures, systems — and everything it doesn't name is optional. This one habit decides whether a job profits or bleeds. The [renovation jobs guide](/guides/renovation-jobs-guide) breaks down what each contract type typically lists.
- **The flat fee is the ceiling, not the floor.** You're paid the agreed amount whether you spend 20% of it or 90% of it on supplies. Profit is literally the fee minus what you spend — the [job profit guide](/guides/job-profit-guide) covers the math.
- **The contract sets the scope of failure, too.** Redos after a failed inspection are scoped to what the client complained about. Fix exactly that, nothing more.

## Budget Discipline: The Permit You Grant Yourself

Between contract and inspection sits the budget, and the demo's best players treat it as a second inspection:

- **Price the job before you buy.** Walk the flat, list every required surface, estimate the cheapest materials that pass, and only then visit the shop. One trip, one plan — return trips are profit leaks. See the [hardware store guide](/guides/hardware-store-guide) for the shopping half.
- **The doorway view is the budget's center of gravity.** Spend where the client's first glance lands — the entrance wall, the bathroom floor — and cheap out everywhere else. The [cost-cutting guide](/guides/cost-cutting-tips) has the full risk table.
- **Keep a reserve.** A job that needs one extra can of paint shouldn't send you back to the store empty-handed. Leftovers from earlier jobs are a free reserve; stash them.
- **Track the daily rhythm.** Fast small jobs build cash and reputation; big jobs build profit per hour. The [upgrade-priorities guide](/guides/upgrade-priorities-guide) is the long-term view.

## How to Fool an Inspection

The demo's inspection is consistent and, frankly, generous. Ways to pass with the minimum possible work:

- **Skip everything out of sight.** Behind radiators, behind furniture, behind closed doors, under cabinets — if it's not visible from the doorway, leave it undone. Both the [painting](/guides/painting-guide) and [tiling](/guides/tiling-guide) guides rely on this.
- **Cover, don't repair.** Holes get posters, cracks get caulk, mold gets paint, water stains get a thick coat. The client checks that things *look* fixed, not that they are.
- **Make the failure invisible, not the fix correct.** A duct-taped pipe passes inspection as long as it doesn't drip. A crooked tile passes as long as the grout covers the gap.
- **Tidy up before the inspection.** Sweep the sawdust, close the paint cans, put tools away. Cleanliness is the cheapest category to ace.
- **Stage the room.** Move furniture back against the walls you skipped before the client arrives. They will not move it again.
- **Keep leftover paint for touch-ups.** Walk the flat yourself right before the inspection and brush over any obvious spots — a two-minute pass saves the whole contract.

## Corner-Cutting Risk Table

Every shortcut in Low-Budget Repairs carries a risk that the inspector notices. This is the risk map, compiled from demo testing:

| Shortcut | Risk | What the inspector notices |
|----------|------|----------------------------|
| Skip hidden walls (behind furniture/radiators) | Very low | Nothing — never checked |
| Skip optional surfaces (rooms not in contract) | Very low | Nothing — not in scope |
| Paint over water stains, one diluted coat | **High** | Stains bleed through thin paint |
| Paint over water stains, thick full-coverage coat | Low | Nothing — stain hidden |
| Dilute paint past ~20% water | Medium | Patchy, streaky walls |
| Mismatched tiles, grouped in visible spots | Medium | Noticeable color mismatch up close |
| Mismatched tiles, hidden behind toilets/furniture | Very low | Nothing — never seen |
| Skip grouting entirely | High | Obvious open gaps between tiles |
| Grout over big gaps instead of recutting | Low | Looks like grout line, passes |
| Duct tape a pipe that still drips | **High** | The client turns the tap. Leak = fail |
| Duct tape a pipe that holds | Low | Function passes; no one inspects the tape |
| Caulk over cracks instead of repairing | Low | Reads as "sealed" from standing height |
| Skip cleaning before inspection | Medium | Visible mess sets a critical mood |
| Throw furniture out the window | Low | Nobody inspects the courtyard (yet) |

The pattern is clear: **shortcuts fail when the flaw is visible or functional, and succeed when the flaw is hidden or covered.** Plan your corners accordingly — see the [cost-cutting tips guide](/guides/cost-cutting-tips) for the full ranked list.

## Tips for Passing Inspection with Minimum Work

1. **Read the contract first (Tab).** Required surfaces only — everything else is optional.
2. **Do the visible work properly.** The doorway view is what gets inspected. Spend your money there.
3. **Hide the rest.** Furniture, grout, caulk, posters, and thick paint are all valid hiding tools.
4. **Fix function, not appearance.** Leaks and drips are the only automatic failures — make the plumbing work, by whatever means.
5. **Tidy up and touch up.** Five minutes before the client arrives beats five hours of redo.
6. **If you fail, fix exactly what they spotted.** Redos are scoped to the client's complaint — don't fix anything they didn't mention.
