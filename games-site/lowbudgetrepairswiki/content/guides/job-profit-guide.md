# Low-Budget Repairs Job Profit Guide — Maximize Profit on Every Job

The money-making guide covers the big picture — the profit formula and every cost-cutting strategy. This guide is the per-job playbook: **what margin to target on each contract type, what eats your profit, and when to turn a job down.** In Low-Budget Repairs you're paid a flat fee, so every job is a cost-minimization puzzle. Based on the July 2026 demo; launch is **August 13, 2026**.

## The Margin Math

Every job runs on the same equation:

```
Profit = Job fee − Supplies − Waste − Redo costs
```

Your only real lever is **supplies** (waste is near-zero if you're careful, and redo costs only appear if you fail inspection). The practical version of the formula:

```
Margin % = Profit ÷ Job fee
```

- **70%+ margin** — excellent. Cheap materials, hidden shortcuts, no waste. The repaint jobs can reach 80–90%.
- **50–70%** — good. The range to aim for on tiling and kitchen jobs.
- **Under 40%** — the job is a trap, you overbought, or the contract demands repairs you can't cheaply fake. Decline or re-plan.

One number matters more than the margin: **profit per hour of your time.** A 40% margin on a huge flat makeover can beat a 90% margin on a tiny repaint, because you finish one big contract instead of three small ones. Read the contract, estimate your time, and compare jobs side by side.

## Job-by-Job Profit Playbook

### 1. Flooded Bathroom Repair — target 55–70%

- **Profit killer:** buying new tiles and pipe sections instead of using the bin and tape.
- **Cheapest acceptable:** duct tape + cheap fitting for the leak, bin tiles for only the visibly broken spots, one diluted can of paint for the stained walls.
- **Hidden savings:** the areas behind the toilet and under the sink never get inspected — leave them.
- **Watch out:** if the leak won't hold, the client's tap test fails the whole job.

### 2. Plumbing Emergencies — target 50–65%

- **Profit killer:** replacing whole pipe lines and repairing plaster after the flood.
- **Cheapest acceptable:** replace only the leaking section with the cheapest fitting that connects; dry, scrape, and paint over the water damage instead of replastering.
- **Hidden savings:** plumbing fees are high because plumbing is "scary" — the fee doesn't know you're using tape.
- **Watch out:** one failed tap test wipes out two jobs of profit. Carry a float before taking these.

### 3. Living Room Refresh — target 80–90%

- **Profit killer:** none, if you follow the [painting guide](/guides/painting-guide). The fee is small but supplies are tiny.
- **Cheapest acceptable:** diluted paint, one coat, skip behind every radiator and piece of furniture, patch holes with posters instead of filler.
- **Hidden savings:** this is the rhythm job. Take every one you see early on; the cash flow funds riskier contracts.
- **Watch out:** a visible hole you forgot to cover is an easy fail. Sweep the room from the doorway before calling the client — that's exactly how the inspector will see it.

### 4. Kitchen Renovation — target 50–70%

- **Profit killer:** replacing cabinets and worktops instead of painting them, tiling more splashback than the contract names.
- **Cheapest acceptable:** paint the cabinet doors (don't replace them), tile only the splashback area listed, hide mismatched tiles under the worktop overhang.
- **Hidden savings:** the worktop is the most expensive surface in the kitchen — if the contract says "refurbish" rather than "replace," sand-and-paint beats buying anything.
- **Watch out:** multi-surface jobs multiply your shopping risk. Plan the full buy list before the store trip (see the [hardware store guide](/guides/hardware-store-guide)).

### 5. Complete Flat Makeover — target 60%+

- **Profit killer:** return trips to the store, premium materials on rooms nobody inspects closely.
- **Cheapest acceptable:** one shopping trip for everything; cheapest materials for the rooms the client walks through last; the "show wall" treatment only in the entrance hall.
- **Hidden savings:** this is the one job where batching pays — every shortcut from the [cost-cutting tips guide](/guides/cost-cutting-tips) applies in the same contract.
- **Watch out:** the biggest scope means the biggest fail surface. Prioritize the rooms the client enters first — the inspection is a walkthrough, and first impressions set the mood.

## When to Decline a Job

Declining is a legitimate profit move. Turn a job down when:

1. **The store can't supply it cheaply.** No bin paint, no matching tiles, the right color is out of stock — a job that requires full-price supplies is a 30% margin job. Wait for a restock.
2. **The contract demands real repairs.** "Replace the bathtub," "new floorboards" — if the fee is too low to cover the mandated spend, the math doesn't work. Skip it.
3. **It's a plumbing job and you're broke.** One failed tap test erases your savings. Build a float with repaint jobs first.
4. **You can't finish before the client's patience runs out.** Time pressure raises the fail risk; a failed inspection pays nothing.

The demo's job board refreshes — another contract will come. A skipped 30% job is usually better than a failed 100% job.

## The One-Shop-Trip Rule

Every store return trip is lost time, and time is the input you can't buy. Before accepting any job:

1. Walk the flat and list every surface the contract requires.
2. Write the supplies list before leaving.
3. Check the bargain bin first and adapt the list to what's there.
4. Buy everything in one trip, including cheap spare consumables (tape, caulk, thinner) for future jobs.
