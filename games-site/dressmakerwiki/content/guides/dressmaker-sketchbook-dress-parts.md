**Dressmaker is a cozy dressmaking simulation developed by Cozy Lives and published by Free Lives, releasing on Steam on September 21, 2026 for Windows and macOS.** The sketchbook is where a dress exists before it costs you anything — and it is the only step in the loop where changing your mind is free.

This guide covers what the sketchbook officially does, how a garment breaks down into parts, and how to plan a design part by part instead of all at once.

---

## What the Sketchbook Officially Does

The official description is specific about this step:

> "Design and colour your dress in your sketchbook, adding fabric samples and appliqué."

Three capabilities, all confirmed:

- **Design** the dress — its form and construction
- **Colour** it — the palette
- **Add fabric samples and appliqué** — the sketchbook accepts material samples, not just colours

That third point is what makes the sketchbook a planning tool rather than a drawing toy. You are not sketching an idea and hoping the fabric matches; you are placing the actual fabric into the design and seeing it.

The sketchbook also sits at the **start** of the loop, before buying cloth. That ordering is deliberate, and it is the single most useful thing about the step.

---

## Why the Sketchbook Is the Cheapest Place to Be Wrong

Compare the cost of a change at each stage:

| Stage | Cost of changing your mind |
|---|---|
| Sketchbook | Nothing |
| After buying fabric | The cloth you bought for the old design |
| After cutting | The whole panel |
| After sewing | The whole garment and the labour |
| After delivery | The customer's reaction |

The sketchbook is the only row in that table that costs nothing. Every design decision you can move into it is a decision you cannot be punished for later.

The practical consequence: **do not buy fabric until the design is settled.** A dressmaker who sketches loosely and shops optimistically will spend most of their coin on cloth for dresses they never make.

## The Parts of a Dress

Pre-release coverage has described Dressmaker garments as being built from **four parts — bodice, collar, sleeves and skirt** [**preview-sourced — [Unconfirmed]** for the September 21 release].

Those four terms are real dressmaking vocabulary, and treating them as the game's part structure is the safest assumption available. What each one does:

| Part | What it is | What it decides about the dress |
|---|---|---|
| **Bodice** | The upper body of the garment, from shoulder to waist | Where the dress sits on the body and how structured it reads |
| **Collar** | The finish at the neckline | The frame around the face — the part people look at first |
| **Sleeves** | The arms | How formal, how covered, how much fabric the design consumes |
| **Skirt** | Everything below the waist | The silhouette — the biggest single shape in the garment |

**Whether the release uses exactly these four parts, or a different set, is not confirmed [Unconfirmed].** No official part list has been published. What is confirmed is that a garment is made of multiple panels and that each panel takes its own fabric — which is a part system by any other name.

## Planning Part by Part

The most common mistake in the sketchbook is designing the dress as one object. It is not one object; it is four decisions that have to agree.

Work through them in this order:

**1. Skirt first.** It is the largest shape and the one that sets the silhouette. Deciding the skirt narrows everything else.

**2. Bodice second.** It has to work with the skirt — a structured bodice over a flowing skirt is a deliberate contrast, not an accident, and it needs to be a decision you made on purpose.

**3. Sleeves third.** Sleeves are the easiest part to add reflexively and the one that consumes the most fabric for the least visual change. Decide whether the design actually wants them.

**4. Collar last.** It is the detail that finishes the neckline, and it is the part where a small change reads the largest.

Then, once the four are settled:

**5. Assign fabric per part.** The official description confirms you control fabric per panel. The [Fabric Guide](/guides/dressmaker-fabric-guide) covers what to put where — a structured bodice and a draped skirt generally do not want the same cloth.

**6. Apply colour.** Colour is confirmed as a sketchbook function.

**7. Keep it coherent.** If parts carry style characteristics that match better when they agree ([Customer Requests](/guides/dressmaker-customer-requests-style-tags)), then a dress built from four unrelated good ideas is a worse dress than one built from one idea applied four times.

## The Mannequin Is the Check

The sketchbook is for planning; the **mannequin is for verification**. The official description confirms you preview the garment on a mannequin before finishing.

Use it as the last gate before delivery:

- Does the dress read as the design you sketched?
- Does it match the customer's event and taste?
- Does it hold together as one garment rather than four parts?

A dress that looks wrong on the mannequin will not look better on a customer. This is the cheapest place to catch a mismatch that survived the sketchbook — the cloth is already cut, but the delivery is not yet made.

## Sketchbook and Decoration

Appliqué appears in the sketchbook's official description, and appliqué also appears in the decoration list. That is worth noting: **appliqué is part of both planning and finishing** in this game.

The practical reading is that appliqué is a design decision, not an afterthought. Planning it in the sketchbook alongside the fabric samples is what the description suggests, and it is consistent with the rest of the game's design philosophy — decide before you commit.
