**Dressmaker is a cozy dressmaking simulation developed by Cozy Lives and published by Free Lives, releasing on Steam on September 21, 2026 for Windows and macOS.** You do not dress a character — you make the garment, from picking the cloth to handing the finished dress to a customer.

This guide walks the full loop in the order you will actually perform it. Everything mechanical here traces back to the official Steam store description; anything that comes from previews or prototype players instead is labelled as such, and anything unverified is marked **[Unconfirmed]**.

---

## What Dressmaker Is — and What It Is Not

It helps to be precise, because Dressmaker's genre neighbours can mislead you.

**It is** a simulation of real dressmaking. The official description says the game is "inspired by real dressmaking", and it means that literally: you arrange and cut pattern pieces from actual cloth, on the grain or on the bias, and the way you do it is what appears on the finished dress.

**It is not** a dress-up game. There is no wardrobe of pre-made outfits to unlock and combine. If you go looking for that, you will be disappointed — and you will also find that the game's central decisions (fabric, grain, layout, seam) pass you by.

**It is not** a farming or life sim with sewing bolted on, either. The loop is dressmaking, end to end.

## The Loop at a Glance

| # | Step | What you are deciding |
|---|------|------------------------|
| 1 | **Sketch the design** | Silhouette, colour, fabric samples, appliqué |
| 2 | **Choose fabric** | Which cloth — and for each panel individually |
| 3 | **Arrange the pattern** | How pieces sit on the cloth: grain alignment, waste |
| 4 | **Cut** | Committing the layout to fabric — irreversible |
| 5 | **Sew** | Feeding panels through the sewing machine to stitch them together |
| 6 | **Decorate** | Buttons, bows, appliqué, lace trims, accessories |
| 7 | **Preview on the mannequin** | Checking the garment before it leaves your hands |
| 8 | **Deliver or sell** | Turn in to a customer, or display in the shop window |

Steps 3 and 4 are where most of the game's depth lives, and they are the two steps beginners rush. More on that below.

## Step 1 — Design in the Sketchbook

The sketchbook is your planning surface. The official description says you **design and colour your dress in your sketchbook, adding fabric samples and appliqué**.

Two things make this step worth taking seriously:

- It is **cheap to iterate here**. Changing a colour or swapping a sample in the sketchbook costs you nothing. Changing your mind after cutting costs you fabric.
- It is how you **match a customer's brief before committing**. If a customer wants a specific look, the sketchbook is where you find out whether your idea reads correctly — not after you have sewn the panels.

Sketchbook work is also where style consistency is decided. Pre-release coverage has described garments as having parts — a bodice, collar, sleeves and skirt — that carry style characteristics, with matching styles reading as more coherent to a customer [preview-sourced, **[Unconfirmed]**]. If that holds in the release, planning your parts together in the sketchbook rather than one at a time will matter.

## Step 2 — Choose Your Fabric

Dressmaker ships with **five officially named fabrics**:

| Fabric | Real-world character |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Cotton** | Breathable, easy to press, forgiving for beginners |
| **Linen** | Crisp and cool, creases readily |
| **Wool** | Warm, holds shape, needs careful pressing |
| **Silk** | Fluid drape with a lustrous finish, slippery to cut |
| **Velvet** | Deep pile with a directional nap — layout matters |

The official description also says you **buy from a wide range of fabrics** and that you can **take full control over what fabric to use for each panel of your garment**. That second part is the interesting one: a dress is not one fabric decision, it is several. A structured bodice panel and a flowing skirt panel do not want the same cloth.

Additional patterned fabrics — among them houndstooth, floral, plaid, damask and gingham — appear in pre-release preview coverage [**[Unconfirmed]**].

> **In-game fabric statistics have not been published.** The right-hand column above describes the fibre in real dressmaking, not hidden game values. Do not treat it as a stat table.

## Step 3 — Arrange the Pattern (Where the Game Gets Deep)

This is the step that separates Dressmaker from every other cozy crafting game, and it is entirely official:

> "Whether your pattern is aligned to the grain or cut on bias, how you choose to arrange and cut out your fabric is what appears on the final dress."

Two decisions live here:

**Grain alignment.** Fabric has a grain — a direction in which it behaves predictably. Patterns are drafted to be placed along that grain. Align them and the garment hangs the way it was designed. Cut against it and the cloth twists, stretches and misbehaves. Dressmaker models this rather than abstracting it away.

**Bias cutting.** Cutting across the grain at an angle (on the bias) gives fabric a fluid, draped quality it does not otherwise have. It is a real technique and, in the game, it is a deliberate alternative rather than a mistake.

**Layout efficiency.** The official description frames this as a balance: how you arrange the pieces is a choice with consequences. Prototype players have reported that the pattern marker turns **green when a piece is aligned with the grain**, and that a piece cut out of alignment has its quality capped [**community-reported from the prototype — [Unconfirmed]** for the release]. If you have played the prototype, that colour cue is likely to be your main feedback signal; the same applies to whatever the release uses in its place.

**A practical rule for your first garments:** prioritise alignment over saving cloth. Fabric is replaceable; a spoiled dress is not. Once you can place pieces correctly without thinking about it, start optimising the layout for less waste.

---

## Step 4 — Cut

Cutting is where your layout becomes irreversible. Everything you decided in step 3 — grain, bias, efficiency — is locked in the moment the scissors move.

Two habits worth forming early:

- **Check every piece before making the first cut.** Once one panel is cut, the layout is committed.
- **Cut one garment at a time until the rhythm is automatic.** Batching multiple garments is an optimisation that punishes mistakes.

---

## Step 5 — Sew the Panels

The official description is direct about this step: **"Feed your fabric through the sewing machine to stitch it together."**

Sewing is the single action every order passes through, so it is where consistency pays off. Independent pre-release reviews have described the quality of the result as depending heavily on **how straight you keep your seams** [**preview-sourced — [Unconfirmed]** for the release]. The skill is not speed; it is steadiness.

Practical advice that follows from that, whatever the release's exact rules turn out to be: slow down, take the machine a panel at a time, and do not chase a perfect score on your first attempt. Speed comes from repetition, not from rushing.

## Step 6 — Decorate

The official description lists the decoration kit:

- **Buttons**
- **Bows**
- **Appliqué**
- **Lace trims**
- **Other accessories** — "wherever you like with complete creative freedom"

Decoration is also where the game's most frequently reported friction point lives. Prototype players have described **certain angles or positions where a decoration refuses to attach** [**community-reported from the prototype — [Unconfirmed]** for the release]. If you hit that, it is a known rough edge rather than something you are doing wrong.

Decoration is where a correct dress becomes a personal one. A garment that satisfies the brief gets paid; a garment that also looks like it belongs in your window gets remembered.

---

## Step 7 — Preview on the Mannequin

The official description confirms you **preview the dress on a mannequin** before finishing. Use it every time.

The mannequin exists so you can catch a garment that misses the brief *before* delivery. A dress that reads wrong on the mannequin will not read better on a customer.

---

## Step 8 — Deliver, or Sell Off the Rack

Two confirmed ways to convert a finished dress into value:

**Turn it in to a customer.** The official description: *"Turn in your dresses to your customers to gain both reputation and coin."* Customers are described as a whole town of people wanting to fill their wardrobes with custom-made dresses **for important events and their individual tastes**.

**Sell pre-made dresses off the rack.** You can *"display your own designs in your shop window and sell pre-made dresses off of the rack."* This is the route that rewards you for making garments on your own initiative rather than only to order.

**On customers specifically:** the official description says you can *"satisfy (or sabotage!)"* the townsfolk. That is unusual for the genre and worth knowing up front — deliberately dressing someone wrong is a supported choice, not a bug.

**On reputation and coin:** these are the two confirmed reward tracks. **No numeric values, reputation tiers or quality-score thresholds have been published [Unconfirmed].** Any guide quoting specific figures before September 21 is estimating.

## Controls and Accessibility

Two of Dressmaker's Steam categories are genuinely relevant to how you play:

- **Mouse Only Option** — the game can be played with a mouse alone
- **Playable without Timed Input** — no section requires you to hit inputs against a clock

That second one is worth repeating, because it changes how you should approach the game. There is no timing pressure in Dressmaker. The difficulty is in the quality of your decisions, not the speed of your hands.

Steam also lists **Custom Volume Controls** and **Family Sharing**.

## Your First Session: A Checklist

1. **Sketch before you shop.** Decide the garment's look in the sketchbook before buying fabric.
2. **Buy less than you think you need.** Fabric bought for a garment you never make is wasted coin.
3. **Align the grain on every piece.** Prioritise alignment over cloth efficiency for your first several garments.
4. **Cut one garment at a time.** No batching until the layout is automatic.
5. **Slow down at the machine.** Straight seams beat fast seams.
6. **Preview on the mannequin before every delivery.**
7. **Keep one garment back for the shop window.** Off-the-rack sales are a confirmed second income stream — use them.
8. **Read the customer's brief twice.** Tastes are individual, and the description explicitly supports getting it wrong on purpose.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Treating fabric as interchangeable.** It is not — the official description makes per-panel fabric choice a core control.
- **Skipping the sketchbook and cutting immediately.** The sketchbook is free; recutting is not.
- **Optimising layout for waste before mastering grain.** Backwards order.
- **Rushing the sewing step.** Reported to be the main driver of garment quality.
- **Never using the shop window.** It is a confirmed income route, not decoration.
- **Believing unverified numbers.** No reputation values or quality thresholds are public.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to play the itch.io prototype first?**
No. The loop is learnable from scratch on launch day. The prototype is a useful preview if you are impatient, but it is an earlier build.

**Is there a tutorial?**
Not documented [Unconfirmed]. The loop is structured enough that the steps above should carry you through the opening.

**Can I fail?**
No failure state has been described. The official framing is a cozy simulation, and customers can be satisfied *or* sabotaged — being wrong is a choice, not a game over.

**Is there a scoring system?**
Quality is clearly tracked in some form, but **no published numbers exist [Unconfirmed]**.

**Can I play with a controller?**
Steam lists a Mouse Only Option; no controller support has been confirmed [Unconfirmed].

**Is Dressmaker multiplayer?**
No. Single-player only.
