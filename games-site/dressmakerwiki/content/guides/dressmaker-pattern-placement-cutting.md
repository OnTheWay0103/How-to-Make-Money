**Dressmaker is a cozy dressmaking simulation developed by Cozy Lives and published by Free Lives, releasing on Steam on September 21, 2026 for Windows and macOS.** Pattern placement is the step where a correct dress and a wasteful one are separated — and it is the step beginners rush because it looks like a formality.

It is not a formality. The official description treats arrangement as one of the two decisions that determine what your finished dress looks like.

---

## The Official Framing

> "Arrange and cut out your dress pattern, preview them on a mannequin and sew them together."

> "Whether your pattern is aligned to the grain or cut on bias, how you choose to arrange and cut out your fabric is what appears on the final dress."

The word doing the work is **arrange**. Arrangement is a player decision with visible consequences. It is not an animation that plays between picking fabric and sewing.

That makes pattern placement a genuine optimisation problem with two competing goals.

---

## Goal 1: Alignment

Every pattern piece carries a grain line, and it has to be placed so that line runs parallel to the cloth selvedge. Get it right and the garment hangs as designed; get it wrong and the panel twists and stretches.

Prototype players report that the game shows this with a colour cue on the marker, and that misaligned pieces have their quality capped [**community-reported from the prototype — [Unconfirmed]** for the release].

This is the goal that **cannot be traded away**. An efficient layout with an off-grain panel is a worse outcome than a wasteful layout with every panel aligned.

Full explanation on the dedicated page: [Grain Direction and Bias Cut](/guides/dressmaker-grain-direction-bias-cut).

---

## Goal 2: Economy

The second goal is using less cloth. Fabric is bought, so waste is money.

Here is the tension, stated plainly: **the most efficient layout is often not the aligned one.** Nesting pieces tightly together usually means rotating some of them to fill gaps — and rotation is exactly what breaks grain alignment.

There is only one correct order of priority:

> **Alignment first. Economy second.**

For your first several garments, do not optimise for waste at all. Place every piece correctly, accept the offcuts, and build the habit. Once alignment is automatic and you no longer have to think about it, start recovering cloth.

---

## A Layout Order That Works

This order applies to any panel set, and it does not depend on unpublished mechanics:

1. **Read the pattern first.** Identify which pieces are large, which are mirrored (left and right versions), and which are small enough to be nested later.
2. **Place the large pieces first.** Bodice and skirt panels are the least flexible. Position them aligned to the grain before anything else claims space.
3. **Place the mirrored pieces as a pair.** A left and right sleeve want the same grain direction. Placing them together makes a mismatch obvious; placing them minutes apart makes it easy to miss.
4. **Nest the small pieces last.** Collars, cuffs and trim sit in whatever space remains, and are the only pieces where a rotation is sometimes worth considering — because a small panel's grain mismatch costs less than a bodice's.
5. **Check every piece before the first cut.** The layout locks the moment the scissors move.

Step 5 is not a formality either. A layout that is 90 percent right and cut is 100 percent wrong.

## Reading the Cloth Before You Lay Anything Down

Where the pieces go depends on the cloth, and the [Fabric Guide](/guides/dressmaker-fabric-guide) covers the fibres. Three properties matter specifically at the cutting table:

- **Does the pattern have a direction?** Stripes, plaids and one-way prints are not rotationally symmetric. Rotating those pieces to save cloth produces a garment with mismatched pattern direction — visible, permanent, and not a quality-score problem you can fix later.
- **Does the cloth have a nap?** Velvet does. Nap is a second direction layered on top of the grain, and panels cut with opposing nap read as different shades.
- **Is the fabric slippery?** Silk is. Slippery cloth is unforgiving of a rushed layout, because pieces shift between placing and cutting.

**How each fabric is modelled in-game is not published [Unconfirmed].** These are the real-world properties the game says it is inspired by.

## Budgeting Fabric for a Commission

Two habits keep cloth costs down without touching alignment:

**Buy for the garment in front of you.** The description says you buy fabric, so unused cloth is coin that is doing nothing. Buying "just in case" is the easiest way to be poor in a dressmaking game.

**Settle the design in the sketchbook before shopping.** The sketchbook accepts colour and fabric samples, so the design can be finalised before money is spent. A design change made in the sketchbook costs nothing; the same change made after cutting costs the whole panel.

**Sketch, buy, arrange, cut — in that order.** Any other order pays for the same decision twice.

## The Offcut Question

What happens to leftover cloth is **not documented [Unconfirmed]**. It may be stored, discarded or unusable on offcuts. Because that is unknown, the safe assumption when budgeting is that an offcut is not recoverable — which means the cost of a wasteful layout is real, and so is the cost of a ruined panel.

That asymmetry is the whole argument for alignment-first: cloth can be re-bought, a cut panel cannot be un-cut.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Dressmaker score how efficiently I use fabric?**
Not published [Unconfirmed]. The official description frames arrangement as determining what the finished dress looks like, which is about the garment rather than about a waste percentage.

**Should I rotate pattern pieces to save fabric?**
Only if grain alignment survives the rotation, or if you are deliberately cutting on bias. Otherwise no — the saving is not worth the cap.

**Can I recut a panel I placed badly?**
You can cut a new panel from new cloth. You cannot restore the fabric already cut.

**Does pattern direction matter as much as grain?**
They are separate properties, and both matter. A piece can be perfectly aligned to the grain and still have its print running the wrong way.

**Is there a way to see waste before cutting?**
Prototype players report a marker colour cue for alignment [Unconfirmed]. Whether the release surfaces a waste figure is not known.

**How much fabric does one dress need?**
Not published [Unconfirmed]. Nobody has a verified per-garment cloth cost before launch.
