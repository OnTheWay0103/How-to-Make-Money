**Dressmaker is a cozy dressmaking simulation developed by Cozy Lives and published by Free Lives, releasing on Steam on September 21, 2026 for Windows and macOS.** Customers are the reason dresses get made. Reading their requests correctly is the difference between a commission that pays well and a garment that sits in the window.

This page covers what is officially confirmed about orders, what preview coverage has reported about style matching, and — because this is the area with the most third-party invention — where the information stops.

---

## What the Official Description Confirms

> "Meet a whole town of people hoping to fill their wardrobes with custom-made dresses tailored specially to important events and their individual tastes."

> "Turn in your dresses to your customers to gain both reputation and coin."

> "Create dresses to impress them, or display your own designs in your shop window and sell pre-made dresses off of the rack."

The confirmed mechanics are:

- There is a **town of customers**, each with an **individual wardrobe** they want to fill.
- Orders are framed around **important events** — an occasion the garment is for.
- Customers have **individual tastes**.
- Delivering to a customer pays **reputation and coin**.
- There is a confirmed alternative to commissions: **selling pre-made dresses from your shop window**.

Everything in this guide is built on those five points.

---

## The "Satisfy or Sabotage" Line

The official short description includes a detail that is genuinely unusual for the genre:

> "…to satisfy (or sabotage!) townsfolk in this cozy crafting game inspired by real dressmaking."

This is worth internalising before your first commission, because it changes what a "wrong" dress means. Deliberately dressing a customer badly is a **supported choice** in Dressmaker. It is not a bug, and it is not necessarily a failure state.

It also tells you something about the design intent: the game is comfortable with you getting a request wrong. If there were a punishing penalty for a mismatch, the store page would not advertise the mismatch as a feature.

## Style Tags — What Previews Have Reported

Pre-release coverage has described garments as being assembled from parts that carry **style characteristics**, with matching styles across parts reading as more coherent to a customer, and stacking the same style tag producing a stronger match [**preview-sourced — [Unconfirmed]** for the September 21 release].

If that holds in the release, the practical consequence is significant: **a garment is not one style decision, it is several that have to agree.** A bodice, collar, sleeves and skirt each carry a style, and a dress that stacks the same one across all of them reads as intentional, while a dress that mixes them at random reads as incoherent.

The parts themselves — bodice, collar, sleeves and skirt — are covered in [Sketchbook and Dress Parts](/guides/dressmaker-sketchbook-dress-parts).

> **This is the report to treat with the most caution.** Style tagging is the mechanic most often described in third-party previews and the least documented officially. No style vocabulary, tag list or stacking rule has been published [Unconfirmed].

## Reading a Brief

Orders in Dressmaker have been described as ranging from **vague to specific** — some customers know exactly what they want, others give you a direction and trust you [**preview-sourced — [Unconfirmed]**].

Either way, two questions do the work, and both come from the official description rather than from any leak:

**1. What is the event?**
Orders are officially framed around *important events*. The occasion sets the register of the garment: how formal it reads, how much structure it needs, whether it should stand out or blend in. This is the filter to apply first, because it constrains everything else.

**2. What is their taste?**
Taste is called out as individual. This is the filter that stops you from repeating the same favourite dress for every customer. A dress you love is not automatically a dress they want.

A third question is optional but useful once you are comfortable: **what is this customer's wardrobe missing?** Customers are described as wanting to *fill their wardrobes* — if you can see what they already own, a dress that complements it is a better answer than one that duplicates it.

## A Method for Each Order

1. **Read the request before touching the sketchbook.** Identify the event and the taste first; design second.
2. **Translate the event into a register.** Formal, celebratory, everyday. This decides structure and fabric weight before colour.
3. **Translate the taste into a palette and fabric.** Colour and cloth carry taste more than silhouette does.
4. **Pick one style direction and stay in it.** If style tags do stack as reported, consistency beats variety.
5. **Sketch it before you buy cloth.** The sketchbook is the free place to discover that an idea does not work.
6. **Preview on the mannequin against the brief.** Not against your own preference.
7. **Deliver, or keep it for the window.** Both routes pay; the window route rewards you for making garments on your own initiative.

Step 7 matters more than it looks. Commissions are guaranteed work; the shop window is where a dress that missed one customer can still find a buyer.

## Reputation and Coin

The two reward tracks are confirmed by name, and that is the extent of it.

**No numeric reputation values, tier thresholds, coin amounts or per-commission payouts have been published [Unconfirmed].** Any guide quoting specific figures before September 21 is estimating them. If you see a number attributed to a Dressmaker customer, ask for the source — there isn't one publicly.

What can be said without inventing anything: reputation and coin are both earned from customer deliveries, and the shop window is a confirmed second route to coin that does not depend on a customer's brief.
