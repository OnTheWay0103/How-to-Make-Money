# Weapon Mods and Upgrading — Zombie Assault Resurgence

Resurgence layers two separate systems on top of the 60-plus weapon roster, and confusing them is the most common way new players waste resources. **Upgrading** makes a weapon you own stronger. **Mods** change how it behaves. The store page draws the same distinction in one line: the crafting system lets weapons and gear "be improved and modified."

This guide covers both, plus rarity, Strongboxes, crafting and dismantling — and gives a spending order that keeps your first ten hours from dissolving into five half-built guns.

## Upgrading versus mods

| System | What it changes | Applied to | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upgrading | The weapon's own numbers | One weapon at a time | Higher output from the gun you already like |
| Mods | Behavioural traits | Weapons and armour | Trade-offs: movement, spread, crit, handling |
| Crafting | Which weapon you own | The armoury | Targeted acquisition instead of luck |
| Dismantling | Inventory into resources | Spare gear | Fuels the other three |

Upgrading is the safe investment. Mods are the interesting one, because mods carry trade-offs — which is exactly what you want from a system once you stop being new.

## What a weapon's stat panel actually exposes

The best way to understand what mods can touch is to look at what the game measures. A Resurgence weapon's stat panel, as captured for the Galvanizer, lists: damage type and damage, pierce and pierce falloff, fire rate, clip size, reload time, crit chance and crit bonus, range, spread, **three separate movement penalties** (idle, firing, aiming), shots to become fully inaccurate, and four status chances (flinch, stun, knockback, knockdown).

That is a long list for a reason. Those are the levers a mod can move, and the reason a mod choice is a build decision rather than a flat buff.

The Galvanizer shows the shape of it. Its firing movement penalty is -11% with a 0% aiming penalty and a -4% idle penalty, spread is 10 degrees, crit is 5% at 1.25x, and it takes 20 shots to reach full inaccuracy. Compare the M2831 power weapon at -40% while firing and 13 shots to full inaccuracy. The two guns sit at opposite ends of the same stat sheet — which is why "mod for damage" is not a universally correct answer.

## The one mod we can name

Public documentation on individual mods is thin, and we will not invent a list. There is exactly one mod named on the official record: the **Movement Speed mod**, which appears in the 0.9.1 demo patch notes as a bug fix — it had not been taking effect, and 0.9.1 corrected it.

That single note is more informative than it looks. It confirms three things:

- Mods alter stats the character also has, not just weapon damage.
- A broken mod was treated as a release blocker for a demo patch, so mods are loadout-critical rather than cosmetic.
- Movement is a first-class stat in Resurgence, which fits a game where Uplink and Cache Recovery demand repositioning under fire.

Treat any other mod name you see in a video or data-mining post as unverified until it appears in a patch note or the in-game interface.

## Rarity, and why it changes the maths

Resurgence uses an **expanded rarity system**, and the community wiki is explicit that mods, the expanded rarity and Subclasses are unique to Resurgence rather than inherited from SAS4. That matters when you look up advice: SAS4 progression advice does not transfer.

Rarity does two things at once. It scales a weapon's damage — the wiki lists damage as a column of values that rise with tier rather than a single number — and it gates whether you can get the weapon at all.

| Weapon | Rarity floor | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Galvanizer (SMG) | Common and higher | Strongboxes |
| Frostbite (pistol) | Common and higher | Steam login, crafting |
| Krachlader (launcher) | Rare and higher | Strongboxes |
| HIKS M2831 (power weapon) | Common and higher | Support boxes only |

The pattern is deliberate. Ordinary weapons start at Common so everyone can get one. Specialty weapons like the launcher start at Rare, so the class stays special. Failure to notice the rarity floor is how players spend an hour hunting a weapon that was never going to drop for them at their current tier.

## Strongboxes, crafting and the drop economy

Three sources feed the armoury, and each solves a different problem.

**Strongboxes** are the general engine. You do not choose the contents, but the pool is broad and the rarity floor per weapon is knowable, so opening them is a probabilistic plan rather than a lottery ticket. Open them as you earn them; a Strongbox in your inventory is a weapon you do not have.

**Crafting** is the targeted route. It is how you get a specific weapon when the pool refuses to cooperate — the Frostbite is explicitly craftable as well as being granted for a Steam login. Crafting is the answer to "I have played thirty hours and still have not seen the one gun my build needs."

**Supply Drops** are separate from both: they are mid-mission, timed, and the only route to Power Weapons. They are not part of the upgrade economy so much as an interruption to it.

## Selling and dismantling

The store page confirms excess weapons can be **sold or dismantled for extra resources**. This is the release valve for a full inventory, and the correct instinct is to use it rather than hoard duplicates.

The distinction worth knowing before you start clearing space: selling converts a weapon into currency, while dismantling converts it into materials. If your bottleneck is crafting, dismantle. If your bottleneck is buying, sell. Either way, a duplicate sitting in storage at Common rarity is worth more as a resource than as a trophy.

## Armour is part of the same system

Mods and upgrading apply to guns **and armour** — that much is confirmed twice, by the game's own description of the mechanics and by the demo build, which shipped Common and Uncommon mods alongside Common and Uncommon armour pieces.

For a Heavy this is the difference between a build and a half-build. Skill points buy survivability in the [class tree](/guides/heavy-class-guide); armour buys the other half. Neglecting armour mods because you are saving materials for a weapon is a false economy in exactly the modes where you need to survive.

## A spending order that works

This is editorial advice, not a system rule, but it holds up across classes:

1. **Pick one primary and upgrade it.** One upgraded weapon beats two unmodified drops. Spreading upgrades across four guns leaves you with four average guns.
2. **Fix the movement penalty first if you play objectives.** A gun that pins you in place loses objectives, and Uplink and Cache Recovery both punish standing still.
3. **Then mod for the role.** Lane-holder mods for a Heavy, accuracy and handling for an Assault, utility for a Medic.
4. **Upgrade armour in step with skills.** Gear and points are two halves of the same defence.
5. **Dismantle before you hoard.** Storage full is a progression stall.
6. **Save materials for the rarity floor you are about to unlock.** Do not perfect a Common weapon the week before Rare starts dropping.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Confusing upgrading with modding.** One raises numbers, the other changes behaviour and usually costs you something.
- **Modding five weapons at once.** Resources do not stretch that far in the first twenty levels.
- **Ignoring rarity floors.** A Rare-minimum weapon will not appear in your current tier's boxes.
- **Hoarding Strongboxes and duplicates.** Both are dead weight until converted.
- **Applying SAS4 crafting advice.** Resurgence's mods, rarity and Subclasses are its own systems.
- **Assuming a damage mod is always right.** Movement and accuracy mods decide objective fights.

## What is still unconfirmed

There is no public mod list with numeric effects for the launch build. The community weapon pages are still being filled in, and the only individually named mod on the official record is the Movement Speed mod from the 0.9.1 patch. We have not invented the rest. When a mod list is published and cross-checked against a second source, this page will carry the full table with effects and stacking rules.

In the meantime, the safest way to evaluate a mod is in the stat panel: apply it, watch which of the exposed fields moves, and decide whether that field is the one your build is short of.
