## Overview

The Marketplace (flea market) in ReStory is a gamble with a skill floor. Most listings are junk, seller ratings lie, and buying the wrong device at the wrong price erases your profit in one repair. But a small set of devices and parts are consistently worth your yen — and knowing which ones is the difference between a growing shop and a shrinking one.

This guide compiles the confirmed price data from launch-week community testing plus the buying rules that keep flips profitable.

---

## The One Confirmed Profitable Build: Autorolla Razor

The **Autorolla Razor** (Motorola Razr-style flip phone) is the only device where building from **brand-new licensed parts** has been confirmed profitable:

| Line Item | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Component cost (new parts) | ~¥22,310 |
| Completed device sale | ~¥26,000 |
| **Net profit** | **~¥3,690** |

Why does this matter? Because the Razor is the *exception that proves the rule*: for nearly every other device, paying retail for new components and selling the finished product loses money.

---

## Why Building From New Parts Loses Money

Community-tested examples from launch week:

| Build Cost (New Parts) | Finished Sale | Result |
|------------------------|---------------|--------|
| ¥21,460 | ¥14,300 | **−¥7,160** |
| ¥105,000 | ¥70,000 | **−¥35,000** |
| ¥22,310 (Autorolla Razor) | ¥26,000 | **+¥3,690** |

You pay retail for parts and sell the finished device for less than the sum of its components — except for the Razor. This is why the game's real economy is **restoration**, not manufacturing: buy broken cheap, repair only what's broken, sell high.

## What Flipping Actually Looks Like

The typical successful flip loop:

1. Find a **cheap, damaged device** on the Marketplace.
2. Buy it — cheap enough to absorb a few bad parts.
3. Disassemble and inspect with the notepad.
4. **Salvage** the working parts for your parts library.
5. Replace only the genuinely broken components.
6. Clean everything (see the [Cleaning Guide](/guides/cleaning-guide)).
7. Reassemble and sell on the Sell Ledge.

**Reality check:** most devices sell for only **~2,000–3,000 yen above their purchase price**. One significant repair cost — a screen, a board — can erase the entire margin. Buy cheap or don't buy.

## Flea-Market Buying Rules

| Rule | Why |
|------|-----|
| **Only buy when price is far below finished sale value** | Most flips net only 2,000–3,000 yen |
| **Assume seller ratings lie** | A "minor cleaning needed" listing can arrive full of broken components |
| **Keep cash reserves** | Spending nearly all your money on one device turns a "profitable" flip into a loss when repairs surprise you |
| **Don't outpace your bench** | Buying donors faster than you can process them just crowds the workshop |
| **Separate customer orders from resale stock** | Mixing them causes missed deadlines and mixed-up parts |

## Salvage: Where the Real Value Is

Even a failed flip is a parts donor. The most valuable salvage targets (community-confirmed):

- **LCD assemblies** — screens are among the most common failures and most expensive parts
- **Button membranes & keypads** — fail constantly, cheap to stockpile
- **Speakers** — small, frequent failures
- **Battery connectors & flex cables** — fragile, regularly damaged
- **Screws & brackets** — free assembly fuel

**The habit that matters:** salvage aggressively from every junk device you buy. A device that loses money as a flip still pays for itself as a parts donor — and stocked parts make future orders take minutes instead of days.

## Which Devices Are "Rare" or "Valuable"?

A definitive value ranking is still being mapped (待验证) — the marketplace's random listing prices make exact figures unstable. What is confirmed:

| Category | Value Profile | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| **Licensed Atari hardware (2600, Lynx, CX40)** | Premium | Real branding, collector appeal; devices appear across the campaign |
| **Consoles (Nony PlayMachine, handhelds)** | High | Highest-paying repairs once licensed; expensive to stock parts for |
| **Phones (Pokia 3310, Autorolla Razor)** | Medium-High | Constant demand; the Razor is the one profitable new-build |
| **Nony Goman (cassette player)** | Medium | High repair payout, but the most involved job — many small parts |
| **Simple devices (Walkie-talkie, Unicorp 99L)** | Low | Cheap to buy, cheap to sell — best as clean-only margin jobs |

**Buying guide:** licensed Atari and console-tier devices are the best flea-market targets when priced low; phones are the safest volume flips; simple devices are only worth it as cleaning jobs, not flips.

## Flipping Achievements

| Achievement | Condition |
|-------------|-----------|
| **Making Money!** | Sell 5 devices after restoring them for resale |
| **Flipper** | Buy any 5 devices online |
| **Garage Sale** | Buy any 25 devices online |

Your flip economy doubles as achievement progress — every restored device sold counts toward **Making Money!**, every Marketplace purchase toward **Flipper** and **Garage Sale**.

## Summary: The Three Ways to Profit

1. **Clean-only restorations** — highest return per yen (reusable tools, zero parts).
2. **Salvage-first repairs** — pull parts from your junk pile instead of buying.
3. **Careful flips** — buy low, restore only what's broken, sell 2–3k above cost, and never manufacture from new parts (except the Autorolla Razor).
